How to start a blog people actually read

A clear, no-fluff walkthrough for choosing a niche, picking a platform, and getting your first posts in front of real readers.

No. 014 8 steps ~6 min read Prereqs: none

Starting a blog sounds simple until you actually sit down to do it. Which platform do you use? What do you even write about? How do people find it once it's live, and how long before it feels like anyone's actually reading? Every one of those questions has a straightforward answer — the trouble is nobody hands you the list in order. Here's the process broken into eight manageable steps, each one building on the last, so you can work through them without second-guessing what comes next.

Step01

Pick a niche you can talk about for years

The biggest mistake new bloggers make is choosing something too broad, like "lifestyle" or "life advice." Get specific instead: budget travel for college students, home coffee brewing, or freelance writing tips.

A focused niche makes content ideas come faster, audience building easier, and helps search engines associate your site with a clear topic. It also tells readers exactly what they're signing up for — a blog about "sourdough for beginners" earns a follow far more easily than one about "food."

Before committing, do a quick gut check on demand. Search your topic on Google and see if forums, YouTube channels, or newsletters already exist around it — that's a sign of an audience, not a red flag. A niche with zero competition often means zero readers looking for it either.

Quick testCould you write 50 posts about this without running out of ideas? If yes, you've found your niche.
Step02

Choose your blogging platform

Your choice depends on how much control you want versus how much setup you're willing to do.

  • WordPress.org — the industry standard, free and highly customizable, with a bit of a learning curve.
  • Squarespace or Wix — beginner-friendly drag-and-drop builders that look polished out of the box.
  • Substack or Medium — best if you want to focus purely on writing, no design or hosting required.
Rule of thumbIf you're not sure yet whether blogging will stick, start on a hosted platform like Substack. You can always migrate to WordPress once you know you'll keep going — it's much harder to go the other way.
Step03

Get a domain name and hosting

If you go the self-hosted route, you'll need a domain name — your blog's address, kept short and relevant — and web hosting, the service that stores your site's files. Most hosts bundle a free domain with your first year, which makes this step cheaper than people expect: a starter hosting plan typically runs $3–10 a month, and the domain itself is often thrown in free.

Stick to a .com if it's available — it's still what people type from muscle memory. If your exact name is taken, tweak the wording before you resort to a confusing spelling or an unfamiliar extension.

Step04

Design with your reader in mind

Your blog doesn't need to look expensive, but it does need to be easy to use. Focus on a clean layout, mobile responsiveness, fast loading speed, and clear navigation. Pick a simple theme and resist over-customizing before you've published anything.

More than half of your readers will land on their phone, often from a search result or a shared link, so test everything at that size first. A theme that looks great on a laptop but takes three taps to find the menu on mobile will lose people before they read a word.

Step05

Write your first posts

A lot of new bloggers freeze at the blank page. The fix is a content plan: draft 5–10 post ideas before publishing the first one.

  • An introduction post explaining who you are and what readers can expect
  • A beginner's guide to a topic in your niche
  • A listicle — these tend to perform well and are easy to structure
  • An honest review or comparison of a tool, product, or method you actually use
  • A "lessons learned" post about a mistake you made, since readers trust specifics over advice
Momentum tipWrite the intro post last. It's easier to explain who you are once you've already got a few real posts to point to.
Step06

Optimize for search engines

Use your target keyword in the title, first paragraph, and a subheading. Write a compelling meta description, use descriptive URLs, link to your own past posts, and add alt text to images.

Before you write, search your intended keyword yourself and skim what's already ranking. It tells you what length, format, and level of detail search engines think readers want — and where you can go deeper than the competition.

ReminderSEO isn't about stuffing keywords everywhere — it's about making the topic obvious to both readers and search engines.
Step07

Promote your blog

Publishing is only half the job. Share posts where your audience already spends time, join niche-relevant communities, start building an email list from day one, and try guest posting to borrow another blog's audience.

Of these, the email list matters most long-term. Social platforms and search rankings are out of your control and change constantly; a list of subscribers is the one audience you actually own.

Step08

Stay consistent

The blogs that succeed aren't necessarily the best-written ones — they're the ones that keep showing up. Set a realistic posting schedule and stick to it. Growth is slow at first, then compounds once you have a backlog working for you in search.

Once a week is plenty to start. A blog with fifty solid posts published over a year will consistently outperform one with five posts and a burnout, because search engines and readers both reward a track record over a burst of effort.

Final thoughts

Starting a blog isn't complicated, it just has a lot of small steps. Choose a niche, pick a platform, set up hosting, write consistently, and optimize as you go. The bloggers who succeed aren't the ones with the perfect plan on day one — they're the ones who published post number one instead of waiting for the right time.
